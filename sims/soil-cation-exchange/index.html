Why do some cations stick tighter than others?

Clay and organic matter carry negative charges. A cation's grip on that surface depends on charge (more charge = stronger pull) and hydrated size (a bigger water shell holds the ion farther from the surface, weakening the pull). The brown line in each picture is the distance from the ion's center to the clay surface — thicker line = stronger attraction.

Notice Mg²⁺ has a smaller bare ion than Ca²⁺ but a larger hydrated size. Its charge is packed into a smaller space, so it pulls in more water — and that thicker shell keeps it farther from the clay, so it is held less tightly than Ca²⁺.

Experiment: which cation does H⁺ knock off?

A clay surface holds equal amounts of Al³⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, K⁺ and Na⁺. Send in H⁺ ions and count which cation gets displaced each time. The weaker a cation is held, the more likely it is to be replaced.

Key ideas. CEC = how many negative charges the soil has to hold cations (a nutrient “bank account” and pH buffer). Base saturation = % of that CEC occupied by base cations (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, K⁺, Na⁺) that don't lower pH. Acid cations (H⁺, Al³⁺) lower pH — Al³⁺ reacts with water and releases more H⁺. Here, CEC below 15 is “low” and BS below 50% is “low”.

Where the H⁺ came from

Root exudate cation exchange

  1. The root pumps H⁺ out of its cells (and releases organic acids).
  2. H⁺ swaps onto a negative site on the clay.
  3. A base cation (e.g. K⁺) is pushed off into the soil solution.
  4. The root absorbs the freed nutrient.

Cellular respiration cation exchange

  1. Root (and microbe) respiration releases CO₂.
  2. CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ (carbonic acid) → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻
  3. The H⁺ displaces a cation from the clay.
  4. The cation enters the soil solution — taken up by roots or leached.
Watch for: each H⁺ that stays on the clay lowers base saturation and pH. Weakly held cations (Na⁺, K⁺) come off most often; Al³⁺ rarely does — but when it does, it reacts with water and releases more H⁺. A 2+ cation needs two H⁺ to replace it (charge must balance).
