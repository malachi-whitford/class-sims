Global Air Circulation: Three Cells, Deserts, and Spinning Winds

The sun heats the equator far more than the poles. Warm air rises, cools, and drops its water as rain; elsewhere air sinks, warms, and dries out the land below. On Earth this sorts the air into three big loops (cells) in each hemisphere, which is why rainforests, deserts, and storm belts sit at the latitudes they do. Earth's spin then bends the winds sideways: the Coriolis effect.

Follow one air parcel around its cell

An air parcel is a blob of air about the size of a house. Watch what happens to its pressure, volume, temperature, and water as it travels the loop in the Northern Hemisphere.

Key ideas: pressure, volume, temperature, and water
  • Rising air expands and cools. Air pressure drops with height (about half at 5.5 km). A rising parcel has less air pressing on it, so it expands, and expanding air cools. Sinking air is squeezed by rising pressure, shrinks, and warms.
  • Warm air can hold much more water vapor than cold air, roughly twice as much for every 10 °C of warming. When rising air cools until it is holding all the water it can (100% relative humidity), the extra vapor condenses into clouds and falls as rain or snow.
  • Sinking air dries things out. It already dropped its water when it rose, and as it sinks it warms, so it can hold far more than it carries. Relative humidity falls, clouds evaporate, and rain is rare. That is why the world's big deserts sit near 30°N and 30°S, and why the poles are cold deserts.
  • Pressure at the ground: where air rises, it leaves low pressure (L) at the surface. Where air piles up and sinks, it makes high pressure (H). Surface winds blow from highs toward lows.
  • The Ferrel cell is the odd one. The Hadley and polar cells are driven by heating and cooling. The Ferrel cell is mostly turned by its two neighbors like a gear between them, and in real life it is messy, full of the storms that bring rain to places like Northern California and Oregon.
  • The pattern shifts with the seasons. The whole set of belts slides north in the Northern Hemisphere's summer and south in winter. California sits near 35–40°N: in summer the subtropical high moves over it (dry summers), and in winter it moves south and lets westerly storms through (wet winters). That is a Mediterranean climate.

Coriolis test

Send a blob of air straight north or south and watch its path along the ground.

How fast is the ground moving east?

Every point on Earth goes around once a day. The equator has the longest circle to cover (about 40,000 km), so it moves fastest. Circles get smaller toward the poles, and the poles just turn in place.

Why the Coriolis effect happens, and why there are three cells
  • Air keeps the eastward speed it started with. Air at 30°N is carried east by the ground at about 1,450 km/h. If it moves toward the equator, the ground beneath it is moving faster (up to 1,670 km/h), so the ground gets ahead and the air falls behind to the west. Seen from the ground, the air curves to its right. That is why the trade winds blow from the northeast.
  • Air moving toward the poles has more eastward speed than the slower ground beneath it, so it pulls ahead to the east: again a turn to the right. That gives the westerlies.
  • Northern Hemisphere: winds curve to the right. Southern Hemisphere: to the left. The effect is zero at the equator and strongest near the poles.
  • Winds are named for where they come from: a west wind (westerly) blows from west to east.
  • No spin, one cell. On a planet that didn't spin, warm air could rise at the equator and flow all the way to the pole in one big loop per hemisphere. On Earth, the Coriolis effect turns the high-altitude poleward flow sharply east by about 30°, where the air piles up, cools, and sinks. That breaks the circulation into three cells.
  • Real winds are much slower than this simple "keep your speed" picture suggests, because friction with the ground and pressure differences also push on the air.
