The Chapman Cycle: How the Ozone Layer Works

High in the stratosphere, ultraviolet (UV) light from the sun splits oxygen molecules, and the pieces build ozone (O₃). Ozone then absorbs the UV-B light that would otherwise reach the ground. Four reactions, first described by Sydney Chapman in 1930, keep making and breaking ozone in a steady balance. Watch them happen one molecule at a time.

The reactions

The Chapman cycle
1O₂ + UV-C → O + OUV-C light splits an oxygen molecule into two free oxygen atoms.
2O + O₂ → O₃ + heatA free atom joins an O₂ to make ozone. A third molecule (usually N₂) carries off the extra energy as heat. That heat is why the stratosphere gets warmer with height.
3O₃ + UV → O₂ + OOzone absorbs UV-B or UV-C and splits. The O atom usually rejoins an O₂ right away (reaction 2), so ozone keeps soaking up UV without being used up.
4O + O₃ → O₂ + O₂Once in a while a free atom hits an ozone instead. Both are lost as ordinary O₂. This slowly removes ozone and balances reaction 1.
Chlorine from CFCs
5Cl + O₃ → ClO + O₂A chlorine atom steals an oxygen from ozone.
6ClO + O → Cl + O₂ClO gives the oxygen to a free O atom and the chlorine is freed again. It is a catalyst: one chlorine atom can destroy thousands of ozone molecules.

What this model leaves out
  • Real ozone is rare: even in the ozone layer, fewer than 10 of every million molecules are O₃. Here there is much more ozone so you can watch it.
  • Most of the stratosphere is nitrogen (N₂). It is left out except as the "third molecule" that carries away heat in reaction 2.
  • Real UV-C is absorbed almost completely. Here a few photons slip through because the molecules are spread out.
  • Nitrogen oxides, bromine, and polar clouds also destroy ozone. The Antarctic ozone hole forms when polar clouds in the cold, dark winter free large amounts of chlorine.
  • The 1987 Montreal Protocol phased out CFCs, and the ozone layer is slowly recovering.
