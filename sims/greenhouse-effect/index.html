A Planet's Energy Balance: How the Greenhouse Effect Sets Temperature

A planet's temperature is set by a balance: sunlight comes in, and the planet sends the same amount of energy back to space as infrared (heat). Some sunlight is reflected right away by clouds and ice. Greenhouse gases absorb some of the outgoing infrared and send part of it back down, so the surface has to warm up before the energy coming in and going out balance again.

How the numbers are calculated (and what's left out)
  • The temperature without a greenhouse effect comes from balancing absorbed sunlight with infrared given off (the Stefan–Boltzmann law): T = [S(1 − reflectivity) / 4σ]¼.
  • The greenhouse effect is added with a simple "gray atmosphere" model, Tsurface4 = T4(1 + ¾τ), where τ is how thick the atmosphere is to infrared. For Earth, τ grows with the logarithm of CO₂ and is set so that doubling CO₂ warms Earth about 3 °C. That value includes the extra water vapor a warmer world holds.
  • Temperatures are long-term balance values. Oceans take decades to centuries to catch up, so the real world lags behind them.
  • Reflectivity is set by hand here. In the real world, melting ice and changing clouds also respond to warming.
  • The moving photons show the idea. The temperatures come from the equations above, not from counting photons.
