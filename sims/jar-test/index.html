The Jar Test: Finding the Right Coagulant Dose

River water is cloudy because of tiny clay and silt particles that are too small to settle and all carry a negative charge, so they push each other away. A treatment plant adds a coagulant such as alum to cancel that charge so the particles stick together into floc that sinks. Too little does nothing; too much wastes money and can make things worse. Operators find the right dose with a jar test: six jars of raw water, six doses, the same mixing the plant uses, and then see which jar settles clearest.

Key ideas: reading a jar test
  • The three steps copy the plant. A rapid mix (about 1 minute, fast) spreads the alum through the water in seconds, before it reacts. A slow mix (about 20 minutes, gentle) lets particles bump into each other and grow into floc without tearing it apart. Settling (about 30 minutes, no stirring) shows which floc sinks.
  • Good floc looks like big, separate flakes with clear water between them, and it settles fast. Pinpoint floc is tiny specks that stay up and cloud the water.
  • Underdose: the particles are still negative and keep pushing each other away, so little floc forms. Overdose: so much positive aluminum sticks to the particles that they turn positive and push each other away again (restabilization). The best dose sits between, where the charge is near zero.
  • Alum uses up alkalinity. Each 1 mg/L of alum destroys about 0.5 mg/L of alkalinity (as CaCO₃) and lowers the pH. Alum works best between about pH 6 and 7.5. Water with little alkalinity, like Sierra snowmelt, can crash to pH 5 or lower, so plants add lime, soda ash, or caustic to keep the pH up.
  • Clear, cold water is the hardest to treat. With few particles, they rarely bump into each other, and cold water slows everything down. Adding more alum doesn’t help: it just overdoses the few particles there are. A polymer floc aid bridges particles together and makes heavier floc.
  • Pick the lowest dose that gives clear water. If two jars are about equally clear, the lower dose wins: less chemical cost and less sludge to haul away.
  • Why it matters: settled water goes on to the filters. Under the Surface Water Treatment Rules, a conventional plant’s filtered water must be 0.3 NTU or less in at least 95% of monthly samples and never above 1 NTU. Particles that cloud the water can also shelter Giardia and Cryptosporidium. Operators rerun jar tests whenever the raw water changes: storms, snowmelt, or lake turnover.

How much coagulant?

100% is “just right”: exactly enough positive aluminum to cancel the particles’ negative charge.

Changing the dose starts over with fresh river water, because you can’t take coagulant back out.

Key ideas: charge, coagulation, and flocculation
  • Why river water stays cloudy. Clay and silt particles smaller than about 1 micrometer would take days to years to settle. Their surfaces are negatively charged and each one carries a cloud of charge around it, so when two drift close, they push apart before they touch. Water like this is called stable.
  • Coagulation means destabilizing the particles. Alum, Al₂(SO₄)₃, releases positive aluminum that forms aluminum hydroxide on the particle surfaces and cancels the negative charge. The charge cloud shrinks, so particles that bump now stick.
  • Flocculation is the slow mixing that follows. Gentle stirring makes more particles meet so small clumps (microfloc) grow into visible floc. Stir too hard and the floc breaks apart.
  • Zeta potential measures the charge near a particle’s surface. Raw river water is often around −20 to −30 mV. Particles stick best near 0 (roughly −5 to +5 mV). Some plants measure it, or a related value called streaming current, to adjust the dose automatically.
  • Too much coagulant coats the particles in positive aluminum, the charge flips to positive, and they repel again. That is why the jar test turbidity curve goes down and then back up.
