Predator–Prey Dynamics

Predators and prey push each other's populations up and down. Explore why populations cycle, what makes cycles calm down or blow up, and how refuges and fear of predators change the outcome.

The equations
Prey eaten per predator: f = aN′ / (1 + a h N′), where N′ = (1 − refuge) N
dN/dt = N [ b / (1 + fear·P) − d − (b − d) N / K ] − f P
dP/dt = e f P − m P

With K = ∞, h = 0, no refuge, and no fear, this is the original Lotka–Volterra model (1925–1926). Adding a carrying capacity and handling time gives the Rosenzweig–MacArthur model (1963). The fear term follows Wang, Zanette & Zou (2016).

How much does one predator eat?

A functional response is how a predator's feeding rate changes with prey density. C. S. Holling (1959) measured it by having blindfolded students "hunt" sandpaper discs on a table.

Risk to each prey individual

Type I: feeding rises in a straight line until the predator is full (filter feeders like Daphnia). Type II: feeding slows as handling time takes up more of the predator's day (most predators, including snakes waiting at cave entrances for bats). Each prey's risk drops as prey get more common: safety in numbers, which can destabilize populations. Type III: predators ignore a rare prey and switch to it once it is common (learning or prey switching). Risk to each prey rises at low density, which helps keep rare prey from being wiped out and steadies populations.
