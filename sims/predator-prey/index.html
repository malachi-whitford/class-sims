Predator–Prey: The Snowshoe Hare Cycle

Snowshoe hares in the boreal forest of Canada and Alaska rise and crash about every 10 years, and lynx, coyotes, and owls follow them up and down. Is the cycle driven by food, by predators, or both? Explore a predator–prey model, then run the famous Kluane experiment and a test of whether fear alone can shrink a prey population.

The paper. Near Kluane Lake in the Yukon, Charles Krebs and colleagues ran one of the largest field experiments in ecology. On 1-km² blocks of forest they added food (commercial rabbit chow), built electric fences that kept out lynx and coyotes (but not owls and hawks, which could fly in), or did both, and compared hare numbers with unmanipulated control blocks for 8 years. During the peak and decline of the cycle, predator exclusion doubled hare density, food addition tripled it, and both together raised it 11-fold, more than the two effects added together. Fertilizing plants increased plant growth but not hares.

Krebs, C. J., S. Boutin, R. Boonstra, A. R. E. Sinclair, J. N. M. Smith, M. R. T. Dale, K. Martin, & R. Turkington (1995). Impact of food and predation on the snowshoe hare cycle. Science 269: 1112–1115. doi:10.1126/science.269.5227.1112

The paper. Can predators reduce prey numbers just by scaring them? Liana Zanette and colleagues protected every song sparrow nest on small islands in British Columbia with electric fences and netting so that no predator could reach them. Then, for a whole breeding season, they played recordings of predator calls to some birds and harmless animal sounds to others. Birds that heard predators laid fewer eggs, fed their chicks less, and produced 40% fewer offspring per year, even though no predator ever touched them.

Zanette, L. Y., A. F. White, M. C. Allen, & M. Clinchy (2011). Perceived predation risk reduces the number of offspring songbirds produce per year. Science 334: 1398–1401. doi:10.1126/science.1210908

Hares and predators, with and without fear

The paper. How many prey can one predator eat? C. S. Holling answered with a famous tabletop experiment: a blindfolded assistant tapped a table to find sandpaper discs ("prey") and set each one aside before searching again. As discs got more common, the number found per minute leveled off because time spent handling each disc left less time to search. In the Kluane study, predators also showed a type III response: they ate few hares when hares were scarce and switched to other prey such as red squirrels.

Holling, C. S. (1959). Some characteristics of simple types of predation and parasitism. The Canadian Entomologist 91: 385–398.
Krebs, C. J., R. Boonstra, S. Boutin, & A. R. E. Sinclair, eds. (2001). Ecosystem Dynamics of the Boreal Forest: The Kluane Project. Oxford University Press.

Type I: feeding rises in a straight line until the predator is full. Type II: handling time limits feeding (Holling's discs); each prey's risk falls as prey get common, which can destabilize populations. Type III: predators ignore rare prey and switch to them once common, so each prey's risk rises at low density. That protects rare prey. Try type III in tab 1 to see how it changes the hare cycle.
