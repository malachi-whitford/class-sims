Four simulations of predators and prey, each built on the organisms and values from a classic study.

At Mukkaw Bay, Washington, the sea star Pisaster ochraceus eats mussels, goose barnacles, and acorn barnacles that compete for space on the rock. Paine removed every Pisaster from a strip of shore starting in 1963: acorn barnacles first covered much of the rock, then mussels and goose barnacles crowded everything out, and the number of species fell from 15 to 8. Rates here are illustrative.
Paine, R. T. (1966). Food web complexity and species diversity. The American Naturalist 100: 65–75.

Huffaker raised the six-spotted mite (Eotetranychus sexmaculatus) on oranges, with the predatory mite Typhlodromus occidentalis, on trays of oranges and rubber balls. With a few oranges, predators found and ate every prey and then starved. In a 120-orange universe with petroleum-jelly barriers that slowed the walking predators and posts that let prey drift on silk to new oranges, the two persisted through three oscillations. Rates here are illustrative.
Huffaker, C. B. (1958). Experimental studies on predation: dispersion factors and predator–prey oscillations. Hilgardia 27: 343–383.

Near Kluane Lake in the Yukon, snowshoe hares cycle about every 10 years. Krebs and colleagues added food, fenced out mammal predators (owls and hawks could still fly in), or both, on 1-km² blocks for 8 years. Food tripled hare density, the fence doubled it, and both together raised it 11-fold. The model is a predator–prey model with default values chosen to give a 10-year cycle.
Krebs, C. J., et al. (1995). Impact of food and predation on the snowshoe hare cycle. Science 269: 1112–1115.
