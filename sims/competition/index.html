Competition: Gause's Yeasts and Paramecia

In the early 1930s, a young Russian biologist, Georgy Gause, tested the new mathematical theory of competition (the Lotka–Volterra equations) with real organisms in glass tubes. His experiments gave us the competitive exclusion principle. Work through his data and his ideas below.

The paper. Gause grew two yeasts in a sugar solution: Saccharomyces cerevisiae (brewer's yeast, fast-growing) and Schizosaccharomyces kephir (slow-growing). He grew each one alone and then both together, and measured the volume of yeast in each tube over time. Both yeasts make alcohol, and the alcohol is what limits their growth, so each species' alcohol slows down the other one too.

Gause, G. F. (1932). Experimental studies on the struggle for existence. I. Mixed population of two species of yeast. Journal of Experimental Biology 9: 389–402. Also in Gause (1934), The Struggle for Existence, chapter IV.

The equations
dN₁/dt = r₁N₁ (K₁ − N₁ − α₁₂N₂) / K₁
dN₂/dt = r₂N₂ (K₂ − N₂ − α₂₁N₁) / K₂

Gause wrote these same equations in 1934, calling them "equations of the struggle for existence." The α values convert individuals of the other species into equivalents of your own species.

The book. Gause also grew three species of Paramecium in tubes containing both bacteria and yeast. P. aurelia and P. caudatum both fed on bacteria floating in the liquid, and when they were grown together, P. caudatum always died out. But P. caudatum could live alongside P. bursaria, which fed mostly on yeast cells that settled near the bottom of the tube. Different feeding places meant less overlap in their niches.

Gause, G. F. (1934). The Struggle for Existence. Williams & Wilkins, Baltimore. Chapter V. The curve positions below are illustrative, not measured values.

How overlap becomes α
α₁₂ = Σ u₁u₂ / Σ u₁²

u₁ and u₂ are the heights of the two curves at each position in the tube. α₁₂ is the overlap divided by how much species 1 overlaps with itself (MacArthur & Levins 1967).

Follow along

  1. P. caudatum vs. P. aurelia. Both feed on bacteria near the top of the tube. Look at how much the curves overlap and at the population graph. Does this match what Gause saw?
  2. Switch to P. bursaria. It feeds lower in the tube, on yeast. What happens to α and to P. caudatum?
  3. Find the limit. Slowly move P. bursaria's feeding position up toward P. caudatum's. At what distance does coexistence break down? Does the answer change if you make both niches wider?
  4. Rescue P. caudatum from P. aurelia. Without changing K, how far apart do their feeding positions need to be for both to survive?
