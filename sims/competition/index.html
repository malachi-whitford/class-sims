On the rocky shore at Millport, Scotland, the small barnacle Chthamalus lives high on the shore and the larger Balanus below it. Balanus dries out high on the shore, but lower down it grows faster and smothers, undercuts, or crushes Chthamalus. The whelk Thais eats barnacles low on the shore. The dots on the chart are Connell's measured yearly Chthamalus mortality at three heights.
Connell, J. H. (1961). The influence of interspecific competition and other factors on the distribution of the barnacle Chthamalus stellatus. Ecology 42: 710–723 (Table VII).

Five warblers of similar size eat insects in the same spruce trees in Maine and Vermont. MacArthur found that each species concentrates its feeding in different parts of the tree. Here, insects grow in each zone of the tree and the birds eat them. Each warbler's feeding zones follow MacArthur's descriptions; the other numbers are illustrative.
MacArthur, R. H. (1958). Population ecology of some warblers of northeastern coniferous forests. Ecology 39: 599–619.

0% = each species keeps to the zones MacArthur recorded. 100% = all five feed everywhere in the tree equally.

The diatoms Asterionella formosa and Cyclotella meneghiniana both need phosphate and silicate. Their half-saturation constants (K), measured by Tilman and Kilham, are used here: Asterionella is better at low phosphate (K = 0.04 µM vs. 0.25), Cyclotella at low silicate (K = 1.4 µM vs. 3.9). Tilman found the winner depends on the silicate-to-phosphate supply ratio. Consumption ratios are illustrative.
Tilman, D. (1977). Resource competition between plankton algae: an experimental and theoretical approach. Ecology 58: 338–348.
