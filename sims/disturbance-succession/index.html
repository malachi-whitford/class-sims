Disturbance & Succession: Sousa's Boulder Field

On the low shore at Ellwood Beach, California, the tops of boulders are covered with seaweeds and barnacles. Winter waves roll the boulders over, scraping them clean and starting succession again. Small boulders roll often, big ones almost never. Wayne Sousa used this natural experiment to test the intermediate disturbance hypothesis.

The papers. When a boulder top is scraped clean, the green alga Ulva and barnacles arrive within weeks. Over the next fall and winter several red algae settle, and if nothing disturbs the boulder, the red alga Gigartina canaliculata slowly takes over, holding 60–90% of the surface after 2–3 years and keeping others out. Sousa measured the force needed to move boulders and sorted them into three classes. Each month, about 42% of small boulders rolled over, compared with 9% of intermediate and 0.1% of large ones. Intermediate boulders had the most species.

Sousa, W. P. (1979). Disturbance in marine intertidal boulder fields: the nonequilibrium maintenance of species diversity. Ecology 60: 1225–1239. doi:10.2307/1936969

Sousa's stabilization experiment

To prove that rolling, not boulder size itself, caused the pattern, Sousa bolted small rocks to the bottom so they could not move, and compared them with loose small rocks. All were cleared at the start.

Follow along
