Connell and Slatyer asked why early species are replaced by later ones and proposed three models. Facilitation: early species make the site suitable for later ones. Tolerance: later species can establish anyway and simply outlast or outcompete early ones. Inhibition: whoever arrives first holds the site, and later species take over only when early ones die. Their test: remove the early species and see whether later ones come in slower, the same, or faster. Rates here are illustrative.
Connell, J. H., & R. O. Slatyer (1977). Mechanisms of succession in natural communities and their role in community stability and organization. The American Naturalist 111: 1119–1144.

Connell photographed permanent coral plots on the reef at Heron Island, Great Barrier Reef, starting in 1962. He argued that high diversity on reefs and in rain forests is not a stable equilibrium: without disturbance, the best competitors would take over, and with constant disturbance, only quick colonizers survive. Diversity peaks when disturbance is intermediate in frequency or size. Coral species and rates here are illustrative.
Connell, J. H. (1978). Diversity in tropical rain forests and coral reefs. Science 199: 1302–1310.

In a boulder field near Santa Barbara, California, waves roll boulders over and scrape them clean. Sousa measured how often boulders flipped: small ones 42% of months, intermediate ones 9%, large ones 0.1%. Small boulders held mostly the green alga Ulva; large ones were taken over by the red alga Gigartina canaliculata; intermediate boulders had the most species. Flip rates are Sousa's; growth rates were tuned to his censuses.
Sousa, W. P. (1979). Disturbance in marine intertidal boulder fields: the nonequilibrium maintenance of species diversity. Ecology 60: 1225–1239.

Tilman showed that many species can share one resource if they trade off competitive ability against colonizing ability. Each patch holds one plant. The best competitor wins any patch it reaches, but it reaches few; weaker competitors persist by getting to empty patches first. Tilman's equations predict how much of the landscape each species holds; the dashed lines show those predictions.
Tilman, D. (1994). Competition and biodiversity in spatially structured habitats. Ecology 75: 2–16.
