Species Distribution Models: A MaxEnt Analysis, Step by Step

One complete species distribution model for blue oak (Quercus douglasii), built from real data: every blue oak record in GBIF and WorldClim climate layers for California. Read it top to bottom like a study. Each step works on what the step before produced, the map follows along as you scroll, and changing any choice re-runs everything after it.

Running results

1Start with the records

Every analysis starts from where the species has been seen. This is a real download of every blue oak record in GBIF: iNaturalist observations, herbarium specimens, and survey data. Raw downloads are messy. Some records have no coordinates, some are misidentified trees in other states, some sit in botanical gardens, and thousands bunch up wherever people hike. Each filter below removes one kind of problem; the map marks what it removed with red crosses.

Cleaning filters

2Describe each record's climate

Each record sits in an 8 km grid cell, and each cell has real WorldClim values: 8 of the 19 "bioclim" variables plus elevation. The model can only learn from the variables you hand it. Many of them measure nearly the same thing: summer temperature and the hottest month's temperature both rise as you go downhill, so they are correlated. Correlated predictors (multicollinearity) make the model split credit between them unpredictably, blur the response curves in step 5, and can mislead the projection in step 7 when warming breaks the link between them. A common rule is to keep only variables with |r| < 0.7, choosing the one that makes more biological sense from each correlated pair. The map now shows the first variable of the pair picked in the matrix, with the records on top.

Candidate variables

"Suggest a set" keeps variables in order of how directly they should affect an oak (summer heat, rainfall, winter cold…), skipping any that correlate |r| ≥ 0.7 with one already kept.

3Decide what to compare them with

Records only say where the oak was found, never where it was absent. So MaxEnt compares the records' climates with background points: a random sample of the area the species could have reached, answering "what climates were available?" Where you draw that area matters. Too big, and the model learns easy differences (oaks aren't in the Mojave). Too small, and it can't learn what limits the species.

Background area

Background points are not absences

An absence says "we looked and it wasn't there." A background point only says "this climate existed in the study area." Some background points land on blue oak woodland nobody recorded. MaxEnt therefore estimates how the records' climates differ from the available climates, not where the species is absent.

4Hold some records back

To judge the model honestly, some records must stay out of the fitting. The model is built from the training records and, in step 6, scored on the testing records it never saw. On the map, black dots are training records and orange dots are testing records.

How to split

Random split: testing records sit right next to training records, so the test is easy. Spatial blocks: whole squares about 150 km across are held out (shaded on the map), which tests whether the model works in places it hasn't seen. That is a tougher, more honest check.

5Fit the model, then tune it

Now the training records meet the background. MaxEnt finds the climates that are more common at the records than in the background. Out of all suitability maps that match the records' averages, it picks the most spread-out one (maximum entropy), assuming nothing more than the data show. Watch the map fill in as it fits.

Model settings
suitability ∝ eλ₁f₁ + λ₂f₂ + …
each f is a feature, each λ its weight

Low β lets the model chase every record (overfitting). High β forces a simpler, smoother model.

Features: linear, quadratic, hinge

A feature is a transformed copy of a climate variable that the model can give a weight. Linear (x) lets suitability rise or fall steadily. Quadratic (x²) allows a hill: best in the middle, worse at both ends. Hinge features are flat up to a knot and then rise; with several knots the curve can bend almost any way. More feature types means more flexible curves and more risk of overfitting.

Weights (λ) and why some are zero

Every feature gets a weight λ. Positive λ: suitability rises with that feature. Negative: it falls. Zero: the model ignores it. Regularization charges a cost for every nonzero weight, so a feature only keeps its weight if it improves the fit by more than its cost. That is why the model "keeps" only some of its features. The fit runs until the weights stop improving and the same features stay in for 15 steps in a row; the badge on the map counts the steps.

Regularization multiplier (β)

β scales that cost. β = 1 is the MaxEnt default. Raising β makes every weight more expensive, so fewer features survive and the curves smooth out. Lowering it lets the model fit fine wiggles in the training records, which often don't hold for new records. Tuning β means fitting several versions and comparing them on held-out data.

Maximum entropy

Entropy measures how spread out a distribution is. Many suitability maps could match the average climate of the training records. MaxEnt picks the one that is as even as possible across the background while still matching those averages (within the tolerance β allows). It makes no claim the records don't support.

The 0–1 output (cloglog)

MaxEnt's raw output is a relative density: how much more likely each background cell is to hold a record than average, summing to 1 across the study area. The cloglog transform rescales it to a 0–1 score. Read it as relative suitability, not a true probability of presence, because presence-only data can't tell how common the species really is.

Permutation importance

Shuffle one variable's values among the training and background points, re-score the model, and see how much training AUC drops. A big drop means the model leans on that variable. Correlated variables can hide each other: shuffle one and the other still carries the signal.

One fit is one guess at how smooth the model should be. To check that guess, the same model is refitted at four values of β with everything else held fixed: same records, background, variables, and features.

6Test it, then draw a line

Now unlock the testing records set aside in step 4. AUC asks: if you pick one record and one background point, how often does the record score higher? 0.5 is a coin flip, 1 is perfect. A big gap between training and testing AUC means overfitting.

Managers usually want a yes/no map, so the 0–1 scores need a threshold: above it a place is called suitable, below it unsuitable. The map now shows that split. Whatever cutoff you choose here is carried, unchanged, into step 7.

Threshold rule
Minimum training presence (MTP)

The cutoff is the lowest score at any training record, so every training record counts as suitable. It gives the most generous map. One mislocated record with a low score drags the cutoff down for everyone.

10th percentile training presence

The cutoff is the score that 10% of training records fall below. It allows for some bad records. By design about 10% of training records fall below it, so testing omission near 10% means the model transfers; much higher means it doesn't.

Max sensitivity + specificity

Sensitivity is the share of training records called suitable. Specificity is the share of background points called unsuitable. This rule picks the cutoff where their sum is highest, the point that best separates records from background.

Omission and TSS

Omission is the share of testing records that land below the cutoff: places the oak really grows that the map calls unsuitable. TSS = sensitivity + specificity − 1, using the testing records and the background: 0 is no better than random, 1 is perfect. Because background points stand in for absences (and some are unrecorded oak sites), specificity is underestimated; compare TSS between models here rather than against other studies. AUC, by contrast, uses every possible cutoff at once, so it doesn't depend on the rule.

7Project it into a warmer California

The last step feeds the same fitted model a future climate. Here the scenario is simple: every temperature variable 3 °C warmer, rainfall unchanged, roughly the warming climate models project for California by late this century. Published studies use downscaled climate-model output (CMIP6) instead.

Before trusting the future map, check where it extrapolates: places whose future climate is unlike anything in the step 3 background. Switch the map to Extrapolation (MESS) to see them in purple.

MESS: the extrapolation map

A multivariate environmental similarity surface (MESS) compares each future cell with the training background, one variable at a time. Inside the background's range it scores how typical the value is (up to 100 at the median). Beyond the range the score goes negative: the farther outside, the more negative. A cell's MESS is its worst score across the variables, so one novel variable is enough to flag it. MESS checks each variable separately; it can miss new combinations of ordinary values.

Clamping

When a future value is hotter than anything in the background, MaxEnt treats it as if it were the hottest value it saw: the response curve is held flat past the edge of the data. That avoids wild predictions, but it is a guess, so suitability in negative-MESS cells is unsupported by data.

Try a model with Elevation in step 2 and one without. Elevation won't warm, so it holds the prediction in place.

Suitable is not occupied. A suitable cell has a climate like the places blue oak grows now. Whether oaks will actually grow there depends on things the model never saw: acorns have to get there (mostly by gravity, scrub jays, and squirrels, a few hundred meters at a time), soils and fire have to allow it, the land can't be paved or farmed, and seedlings have to survive grazing and drought. Blue oaks live for centuries, so adult trees can persist in a "lost" cell long after new seedlings stop establishing there. Gained area is where conditions may open up, not where the species will be.

Uncertainty. This map is one model run under one scenario. Different climate models, emission paths, background choices, β values, and threshold rules each shift the result; the threshold rule alone can change today's suitable area a lot (compare the rules in step 6). Published projections run many climate models and report where they agree. Treat the change map as a direction and a magnitude to investigate, and treat purple MESS areas as guesses.

Feature functions: the building blocks of a response curve

MaxEnt can't draw any curve it likes. It builds each response curve out of features: simple functions of a climate variable. Each feature gets a weight, λ (lambda). The model adds up weight × feature for every feature, then raises e to that total. Positive weights push suitability up where the feature is large; negative weights push it down.

suitability ∝ eλ₁·x + λ₂·x² + λ₃·hinge(x) + …
Weights
Try a shape

A linear feature alone gives a curve that only rises or only falls. Add a quadratic with a negative weight and you get a hill: a best value in the middle. Hinge features are zero up to a knot and then rise in a straight line (MaxEnt also uses reverse hinges that fall before the knot). With many knots, hinges can bend the curve almost anywhere, which is why "+ Hinge" in step 5 fits the records most closely and overfits most easily.

β, the regularization multiplier: how much wiggle to allow

Every nonzero weight costs a penalty, and β sets how big that penalty is. With a small β, weights are cheap: the model keeps lots of features and its curve follows every bump in the training records, including bumps that are only noise. With a large β, only features that really earn their keep survive, so the curve is smooth. This demo fits a one-variable MaxEnt model to the training records and background from your analysis, so you can watch β work on real data.

MaxEnt's default is β = 1. Studies usually compare a range, such as 0.5 to 4, and keep the one that predicts held-out records best (step 5 does this for the full model).

AUC, thresholds, omission, and TSS

A model gives every place a score. Records should score high and the background mostly low, but the two always overlap. AUC measures how well the scores separate the two groups, across every possible cutoff at once: it is the chance that a random record outscores a random background point. A threshold picks one cutoff to make a yes/no map, and the scores at that cutoff follow from it: sensitivity (records called suitable), specificity (background called unsuitable), omission (records missed = 1 − sensitivity), and TSS (sensitivity + specificity − 1).

Scores to use

Slide the separation to 0 and AUC falls to 0.5: the model can't tell records from background. Then slide the threshold. Moving it up makes the map stricter: specificity rises, but more real records are missed (omission). No single cutoff is "right"; the rules in step 6 are different answers to how many records you're willing to miss. A commonly cited guide reads AUC above 0.9 as excellent and 0.7–0.9 as useful, but AUC also rises with a bigger background (step 6 shows the same model scoring differently against two backgrounds), so compare it only between models with the same background.

MESS: is this climate inside what the model learned?

A model only knows the climates in its background. MESS (multivariate environmental similarity surface) scores a new value against that background, one variable at a time. Near the middle of the background the score approaches 100. At the edge of the background it reaches 0. Past the edge it goes negative, and the further out, the more negative. A map cell takes the lowest score of all its variables, so one unfamiliar variable is enough to flag it.

In step 7, every temperature variable moves 3 °C warmer. For the hottest places in California, that pushes summer temperatures past anything in the background, so their MESS turns negative. MaxEnt still gives those places a score by clamping: treating the new value as if it were the hottest one it saw. Those predictions rest on no data, which is what the purple on the MESS map warns you about.
