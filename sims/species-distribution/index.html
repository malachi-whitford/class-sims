Species Distribution Models: How MaxEnt Works

Biologists have found a species at a handful of places. Where else could it live? MaxEnt answers by comparing the climate at those places with the climate available across the landscape, then painting every place by how much it resembles the sites where the species was found.

The species is the American pika (Ochotona princeps), a small rabbit relative of high Sierra talus slopes that overheats easily. The landscape is a made-up slice of the southern Sierra Nevada, from the Central Valley over the crest to the dry east side. Because we built this world, we know where pikas really can live, so you can check the model against the truth, which real biologists never get to do.

What the model sees: climate space

Every map cell is a point here, placed by its temperature and rainfall instead of its location. MaxEnt never sees the map, only this. It looks for climates that are more common among the records than in the background.

How MaxEnt builds the map

  1. Presence records. Places where the species was seen. There are no absences: a blank spot might be unsuitable, or nobody looked.
  2. Background. Thousands of random points from the area the species could reach. They describe what climates are available, not where the species is absent.
  3. Compare. For each climate, MaxEnt asks: is it more common at the records than in the background? Out of every map that matches the records' averages (their mean temperature, mean rainfall, and so on), it picks the one that is most spread out, the maximum entropy one, so it assumes nothing the data do not support.
    suitability ∝ eλ₁f₁ + λ₂f₂ + λ₃f₃ + …
    Each f is a feature (temperature, temperature², a hinge…) and each λ is a weight the model learns.
  4. Regularize. The match to the records is allowed to be loose, by an amount set by β. Features that do not earn their keep get a weight of exactly zero and drop out.
  5. Project. Feed the same equation new climate layers, such as a future climate, to get a new map. Climates hotter than anything in the background are clamped to the hottest value the model saw.
