Species Distribution Models: A MaxEnt Analysis, Step by Step

Run a full species distribution model for blue oak, from raw records to a climate-change map, the same way a published study would. Because this California is simulated, you can check every choice against the oak's true range, which real biologists never get to see.

1. Get and clean the records

Records come from herbaria, surveys, and apps like iNaturalist, usually downloaded from GBIF. Raw downloads are messy: repeat records, typos that drop a point in the ocean, and trees planted in yards and parks far from where the species grows wild.

2. Choose climate variables

Studies start from a set of climate layers like WorldClim's 19 "bioclim" variables. Many of them measure nearly the same thing. Annual temperature and July temperature both rise as you go downhill, so they are correlated.

Correlated predictors (multicollinearity) make the model split credit between them unpredictably. The response curves become hard to read, and the projection can go wrong when climate change breaks the relationship between them. A common rule is to keep only variables with |r| < 0.7, choosing the one that makes more biological sense from each correlated pair.

Tap a square in the correlation matrix to see that pair plotted.

3. Define the study area and background

MaxEnt has no absences. Instead it compares the records with background points, a random sample of the area the species could have reached. The background answers "what climates were available?"

Where you draw that area matters. Too big, and the model learns easy differences (oaks aren't in the Mojave). Too small, and it can't learn what limits the species.

4. Split into training and testing records

To judge the model honestly, hold some records back. The model is built from the training records and scored on the testing records it never saw.

Random split: testing records sit right next to training records, so the test is easy. Spatial blocks: whole 200 km squares are held out, which tests whether the model works in places it hasn't seen. That is a tougher, more honest check.

5. Fit the model

MaxEnt finds the climates that are more common at the training records than in the background. Out of all maps that match the records' averages, it picks the most spread-out one (maximum entropy), assuming nothing more than the data show.

suitability ∝ eλ₁f₁ + λ₂f₂ + …

Feature types

Features are the shapes a response curve may take: straight lines, hills, or flexible bends.

β penalizes complexity. Low β lets the model chase every record (overfitting). High β forces a simpler model. Studies often test several β values and pick the best.

6. Evaluate and threshold

AUC asks: if you pick one record and one background point, how often does the record score higher? 0.5 is a coin flip, 1 is perfect. Compare training AUC with testing AUC: a big gap means overfitting.

Managers often want a yes/no map, so we pick a threshold. Above it the place is called suitable, below it unsuitable.

10th percentile: 90% of training records fall above the cutoff. Max sensitivity + specificity: the cutoff that best separates records from background.

7. Project to a future climate

Feed the same model a future climate (here, July 3.5 °C warmer and January 2 °C warmer, about what models give for California late this century under high emissions) and compare the maps.

Watch for extrapolation: future climates hotter than anything in the background. The model has never seen them, so MaxEnt clamps them to the most extreme value it saw. Treat those areas with caution.

Try models that use Elevation, or Annual temperature instead of July temperature. Elevation won't warm, so the model can't see the change at all.
