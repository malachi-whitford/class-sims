What Makes a Greenhouse Gas? Light Meets the Air

Shine different kinds of light on the gases in our air. Some light passes straight through. Some makes a molecule spin, vibrate, glow, or break apart. Greenhouse gases are the molecules that absorb infrared (heat) radiation and then give it off again in a random direction, often back toward the ground.

How can vibrate?

Every molecule can only vibrate in a few set ways. Infrared can grab a vibration only if it makes the molecule’s charge lopsided. The gold arrow shows how lopsided the charge is at each moment; a gray dot means perfectly balanced.

Key ideas: what makes a greenhouse gas
  • Light is a wave of electric field. As it passes a molecule, the field pushes positive charge one way and negative charge the other, flipping back and forth. Infrared flips about 30 trillion times a second.
  • Atoms don’t share electrons evenly. Oxygen pulls harder than carbon or hydrogen, so in CO₂ and H₂O the O atoms are slightly negative (δ−) and the others slightly positive (δ+). The field has something to push on.
  • The push has to match a vibration. Infrared is absorbed when the flipping field matches the rhythm of a vibration that makes the charge lopsided. CO₂’s bend (wavelength near 15 µm) sits right where Earth gives off lots of heat, which is why CO₂ matters so much.
  • N₂ and O₂ can’t play. Two identical atoms share electrons evenly. No δ+ or δ−, so stretching changes nothing the field can grab. Together they are 99% of the air but trap no heat.
  • Rule of thumb: molecules with three or more atoms (CO₂, H₂O, CH₄, O₃, N₂O) or two different atoms (CO) can absorb infrared. Water vapor causes the largest share of the natural greenhouse effect; CO₂ is the one we are changing most.

What this model leaves out
  • Real greenhouse gases each absorb only certain infrared wavelengths. Here every CO₂ and H₂O molecule absorbs every infrared photon that hits it.
  • Real air has far more molecules, and CO₂ is only about 0.04% of them. The molecules are spread out here so you can follow single photons.
  • Clouds, ice, and oceans reflect, store, and move heat. They are left out. The temperature uses a simple energy balance: the ground warms until the infrared it gives off balances the sunlight plus the infrared sent back to it.
  • Without any greenhouse gases, Earth's average temperature would be about −18 °C (0 °F). Today it is about 15 °C (59 °F).
