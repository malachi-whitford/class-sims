At the same water content (15%), why is water easy to take up from sand but almost impossible from clay?

Clay has huge surface area, so at 15% almost all its water is a thin film stuck to particles (very negative Ψm, below the wilting point). Sand at 15% still holds water in pores near field capacity (Ψm close to 0).

Why can salty irrigation water cause drought stress even in moist soil?

Salts lower soil Ψs. If Ψsoil drops below Ψroot, the plant can't absorb water (physiological drought). As soil dries, salts concentrate and Ψs drops even further.

Available water = field capacity − wilting point. Loams and silt loams hold the most available water; sand holds little; clay holds a lot, but much of it is unavailable.

Define field capacity and permanent wilting point in terms of water potential.

Field capacity: water left after gravity drains a saturated soil for 1–3 days, about Ψm = −0.033 MPa (−1/3 bar). Permanent wilting point: plants can no longer extract water and do not recover overnight, about −1.5 MPa (−15 bar).

What is the difference between capillary water and hygroscopic water?

Capillary water is held in small pores by cohesion and adhesion, between field capacity and about −3.1 MPa; most of it (down to −1.5 MPa) is available to plants. Hygroscopic water is a very thin film bonded tightly to particle surfaces (below about −3.1 MPa). Plants can never use it.

Why is gravitational water not considered "available," even though roots can take some of it up?

It drains out of the root zone within a day or two, and while it's there it fills the pores that roots need for oxygen (see the Redox tab). It also carries nitrate down to groundwater.

Irrigation managers often water when about half of the available water is used. Why?

Below that point (the plant stress zone) water is held more tightly, so plants close stomata and growth slows, even though they are not yet wilting.

What is the difference between infiltration and permeability?

Infiltration is water entering the soil surface. Permeability (hydraulic conductivity) is how easily water moves through the soil once inside. A crusted or compacted surface can have slow infiltration even if the soil below is permeable.

Why does the infiltration rate start high and then slow down?

At first, dry soil pulls water in with strong matric potential (Ψm) as well as gravity. As the wetted zone gets deeper, the pull from dry soil is farther away, so the rate drops toward a steady rate set by the soil's permeability.

How is slow infiltration connected to flooding and dead zones?

Water that can't infiltrate becomes runoff. Runoff causes flooding and erosion, and carries fertilizer (N and P) to rivers and the ocean, feeding algae blooms that use up oxygen (eutrophication → dead zones). Fast-draining sandy soils have the opposite problem: nitrate leaches down to groundwater.

The redox ladder

What do "oxidation" and "reduction" mean? (OIL RIG)

Oxidation Is Loss of electrons; Reduction Is Gain. Microbes oxidize organic matter (it loses electrons) and must hand those electrons to an electron acceptor, which is reduced. O₂ is the best acceptor; when it runs out they use NO₃⁻, then Mn⁴⁺, Fe³⁺, SO₄²⁻, and finally CO₂.

Why does saturating a soil remove its oxygen?

Water fills the air-filled pores, and O₂ diffuses about 10,000 times more slowly through water than through air. Roots and microbes use up what's left within about a day.

How can you tell from a soil pit that a soil is often waterlogged?

Look for gray (gleyed) colors where Fe³⁺ was reduced to soluble Fe²⁺ and washed away, and red-orange mottles where Fe²⁺ was re-oxidized when the soil drained. These redoximorphic features are used to identify wetland (hydric) soils.

Why is a waterlogged field a problem for nitrogen fertilizer?

Denitrifying bacteria use NO₃⁻ as an electron acceptor and convert it to N₂ (and N₂O, a greenhouse gas) that escapes to the air, so the fertilizer is lost.
