Water Potential in Plants

Ψ = Ψs + Ψp + Ψg + Ψm

Water always moves from higher (less negative) water potential to lower (more negative) water potential. Units: megapascals (MPa). Pure water at sea level = 0 MPa.

Ψs Solute (osmotic) potentialΨs = −iCRT. Dissolved solutes always make it negative. More solute → more negative.
Ψp Pressure potentialPositive when a cell wall pushes back (turgor). Negative when water is pulled (tension in xylem).
Ψg Gravity potentialΨg = ρgh ≈ 0.0098 MPa per meter of height. Matters in tall trees.
Ψm Matric potentialWater held by adhesion to surfaces (soil particles, cell walls). Always ≤ 0; very negative in dry soil.

Check: A cell has Ψs = −0.7 MPa and Ψp = +0.3 MPa. It is placed in pure water. Which way does water move?

Cell Ψ = −0.7 + 0.3 = −0.4 MPa. Pure water = 0 MPa. Water moves into the cell (0 → −0.4) until rising turgor pressure makes the cell Ψ = 0.

Check: Why does adding salt to the outside solution make a plant cell lose water?

Salt makes the outside Ψs (and therefore Ψ) more negative than the cell. Water moves from the cell (higher Ψ) to the solution (lower Ψ): plasmolysis.

What is the Casparian strip and why does it matter?

A waxy (suberin) band in the walls of endodermis cells. It blocks the apoplast (wall) pathway, forcing water and minerals to cross a cell membrane. This lets the plant control which minerals enter the xylem.

Why can over-fertilizing "burn" a plant even when the soil is wet?

Excess salts make soil Ψs very negative. If Ψsoil drops below Ψroot, water flows out of the root even though the soil is moist.

Why doesn't a plant just keep pumping ions to pull water from very dry soil?

Below about −1.5 MPa (the permanent wilting point) soil water is held so tightly that most plants cannot lower their own Ψ enough, and soil hydraulic conductance falls sharply.

How high can adhesion (capillarity) alone lift water in a 20 µm-radius vessel?

h = 2γcosθ / (ρgr) ≈ 1.5×10⁻⁵ / r ≈ 0.74 m. Far short of a 30 m tree, so a pulling force (transpiration + cohesion) is needed.

Explain the cohesion-tension theory in one sentence.

Evaporation from leaves creates negative pressure (tension) that pulls a continuous column of water, held together by hydrogen bonds (cohesion) and to vessel walls (adhesion), up from the roots.

Why do trees in cold or dry climates often have narrower vessels?

Narrow vessels are less likely to cavitate (form air bubbles) under high tension or freeze-thaw, trading speed for safety.

Environment

Light opens stomata (guard cells take up K⁺ and swell).
Why is the water potential of air so much more negative than the leaf?

Ψair = (RT/V̄w)·ln(RH). Even at 90% RH, air is about −14 MPa; at 50% RH about −94 MPa. That huge drop happens at the stomata, where liquid water turns to vapor.

Which conditions increase transpiration?

Low humidity, high temperature, wind (removes the humid boundary layer), bright light (open stomata), and moist soil. Humid air, darkness, and drought (stomata close) decrease it.

Why do stomata close during drought, and what is the trade-off?

Closing stomata prevents leaf Ψ from becoming so negative that xylem cavitates or cells wilt, but it also blocks CO₂ uptake, so photosynthesis slows.
