Inversion Layers: Why the Central Valley Traps Air Pollution

Normally air gets colder as you go up, so warm, polluted air near the ground rises and spreads out. During a temperature inversion, a layer of warmer air sits above cooler air like a lid. Pollution rises only until it hits that lid. In the San Joaquin Valley, mountains wall in the other sides, so pollution from traffic, farms, and homes builds up day after day.

About the San Joaquin Valley, and what this model leaves out
  • The valley is a bowl: the Coast Ranges to the west, the Sierra Nevada to the east, and the Tehachapi Mountains to the south. Air that can't rise over the lid has few ways out.
  • In the American Lung Association's "State of the Air" 2026 report, Bakersfield ranked worst in the U.S. for year-round particle pollution, Fresno ranked 4th, and Visalia tied for 5th.
  • Winter: long, clear nights cool the ground, and cold, heavy air pools on the valley floor under warmer air. Damp cold air forms tule fog. Wood smoke, diesel exhaust, and ammonia from farms and dairies make fine particles (PM2.5).
  • Summer: a high-pressure system makes air high up sink and warm, forming a lid hundreds of meters up. Hot sun turns trapped car and farm emissions into ground-level ozone. This model tracks particles only.
  • Heights are stretched about 20 times compared with distances so you can see the lid. The air "puffs" stand for pollution, not individual molecules. AQI uses the EPA's PM2.5 scale, but its values are scaled for teaching, not forecasting.
