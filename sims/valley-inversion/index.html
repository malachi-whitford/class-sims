The big idea: what makes an inversion?
  • Normally, air gets colder with height (about 6.5 °C per km), because the ground is what heats the air, and rising air expands and cools.
  • An inversion is any layer where air gets warmer with height. That happens in two main ways: the air near the ground gets cooled from below, or the air higher up gets warmed from above.
  • Cooled from below (night cooling, or "radiation inversion"): on long, clear, calm nights the ground radiates heat to space as infrared and cools fast. It chills the air touching it. Cold air is dense, so it stays put at the bottom, and cold air from the foothills drains down and pools on the valley floor. Clouds (a blanket that sends infrared back down) or wind (which stirs warmer air down) prevent it.
  • Warmed from above (sinking air, or "subsidence inversion"): in a high-pressure system, air high up sinks slowly. Sinking air gets squeezed by rising pressure and warms about 10 °C for every kilometer it drops. It arrives warmer than the air normally found at that height and settles on top of the cooler valley air like a lid.
  • Why the San Joaquin Valley gets both: it's a bowl ringed by mountains that block wind, winter nights are long, and from spring to fall the Pacific High (the sinking branch of the global Hadley cell) sits off California. In winter, a high-pressure ridge can do both at once: sinking air warms the air above while long nights chill the air below, making a strong lid that can last for weeks.
  • A third way: a warm air mass can slide in over a pool of cold air that is already sitting in the valley (warm-air advection). The result is the same, warm over cold.
  • What breaks an inversion: sunshine heating the ground (strong in summer, weak in winter), wind, or a storm that brings cold air aloft.

About the San Joaquin Valley, and what this model leaves out
  • The valley is a bowl: the Coast Ranges to the west, the Sierra Nevada to the east, and the Tehachapi Mountains to the south. Air that can't rise over the lid has few ways out.
  • In the American Lung Association's "State of the Air" 2026 report, Bakersfield ranked worst in the U.S. for year-round particle pollution, Fresno ranked 4th, and Visalia tied for 5th.
  • Winter: long, clear nights cool the ground, and cold, heavy air pools on the valley floor under warmer air. Damp cold air forms tule fog. Wood smoke, diesel exhaust, and ammonia from farms and dairies make fine particles (PM2.5).
  • Summer: a high-pressure system makes air high up sink and warm, forming a lid hundreds of meters up. Hot sun turns trapped car and farm emissions into ground-level ozone. This model tracks particles only.
  • Heights are stretched about 20 times compared with distances so you can see the lid. The air "puffs" stand for pollution, not individual molecules. AQI uses the EPA's PM2.5 scale, but its values are scaled for teaching, not forecasting.
