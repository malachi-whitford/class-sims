Island Biogeography: The Mangrove Island Experiments

MacArthur and Wilson (1967) proposed that the number of species on an island is a balance between new species arriving and resident species dying out. Two years later, Daniel Simberloff and E. O. Wilson tested the idea by wiping out every insect and spider on tiny mangrove islands in the Florida Keys and watching them come back.

The paper. In 1966–67, Simberloff and Wilson chose small islands made only of red mangrove trees, about 11–12 m across, at different distances from larger mangrove "source" areas. They counted every arthropod species on each island, covered it with a tent, and fumigated it with methyl bromide, which killed the arthropods but not the trees. Then they recounted for two years. All but the most distant island (E1, 533 m away) were back to about their original number of species within about 250 days, and the nearest (E2, 2 m away) had the most. The species lists kept changing even after the numbers settled.

Simberloff, D. S., & E. O. Wilson (1969). Experimental zoogeography of islands: the colonization of empty islands. Ecology 50: 278–296. doi:10.2307/1934856

How the model works

Each day, every pool species not on an island has a small chance to arrive (lower for far islands), and every species on an island has a small chance to die out there (higher for small islands). Immigration per day = 0.095 + 0.26·e−distance/86 m when the island is empty; extinction per day = 0.15 × (11.5 m ÷ diameter) when every pool species is present. These values were tuned so the model's equilibria match the paper's counts.

  2. Stop at about day 250 and compare each island with the dashed line (its count before fumigation). Simberloff and Wilson found every island except E1 back near its original count by then.
  3. Keep running to day 700. Does E1 catch up? The real E1 still had not fully recovered after two years.
  4. Check the turnover column. The real islands gained and lost species at roughly 0.05–0.5 species per day even after the counts leveled off. Does the model?

  6. Something the model misses: on the real islands, species numbers often rose slightly above the original count and then fell back as populations grew and species interacted. Why can't this model do that?

MacArthur–Wilson graph

The paper. Does island area matter by itself, or only because big islands have more kinds of habitat? Simberloff answered with chainsaws: he censused the arthropods on eight small mangrove islands, then had crews cut away parts of some islands, leaving the same kind of habitat on a smaller area. Species numbers fell on the islands he shrank and stayed about the same on untouched control islands. Area alone changed the number of species.

Simberloff, D. (1976). Experimental zoogeography of islands: effects of island size. Ecology 57: 629–648. doi:10.2307/1936179
