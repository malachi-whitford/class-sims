Island Biogeography

Four simulations of how area and isolation set the number of species, each built on a classic study.

The 1883 eruption of Krakatau, about 40 km from Java and Sumatra, wiped out all life on the island. Resident land birds were counted at 13 species in 1908, 27 in 1919–21, and 27 in 1932–34, while some species disappeared and others arrived between counts. MacArthur and Wilson argued that the count had reached an equilibrium where new arrivals balance extinctions. Rates here are illustrative.
MacArthur, R. H., & E. O. Wilson (1963). An equilibrium theory of insular zoogeography. Evolution 17: 373–387.

In the Florida Keys, Simberloff and Wilson tented small red-mangrove islands and fumigated them, killing every insect and spider. Islands had held 20 to 35 arthropod species, fewest on the island farthest from the mainland mangroves (E1, 533 m) and most on the nearest (E2, 2 m). Within about a year, most islands were back to their original number of species, but not the same species. Rates here are fitted to the original counts.
Simberloff, D. S., & E. O. Wilson (1969). Experimental zoogeography of islands: the colonization of empty islands. Ecology 50: 278–296.

Newmark treated 14 western North American national parks as "land-bridge islands": once surrounded by similar habitat, now cut off by farms, towns, and roads. Using historical records of rabbits and hares, carnivores, and hoofed mammals, he found that smaller parks had lost more species (up to 43% in Lassen Volcanic) and only the largest park group kept all of its species. Rates here are illustrative.
Newmark, W. D. (1987). A land-bridge island perspective on mammalian extinctions in western North American parks. Nature 325: 430–432.

Haddad and 23 colleagues mapped the world's forests and found that about 20% of forest is within 100 m of an edge and 70% within 1 km. In seven long-term fragmentation experiments on five continents, fragmentation cut biodiversity by 13 to 75%, and the losses kept growing for years after the habitat was broken up. This landscape is 3 × 3 km; species needs are illustrative.
Haddad, N. M., et al. (2015). Habitat fragmentation and its lasting impact on Earth's ecosystems. Science Advances 1: e1500052.
